# CalmGuide Crisis Mode: Few-Shot Examples

These validated examples demonstrate the expected four-section response format, proper safety guardrail adherence, and tone. They are based on guidance from the Alzheimer's Association, the National Institute on Aging, and established dementia care best practices (validation therapy, person-centered care).

Each example uses a fictional patient profile and shows the complete response a caregiver should receive.

---

## Example 1: Nighttime Wandering (Middle Stage)

**Situation:** "Mom just put on her coat and is trying to leave the house. It's 2am and she says she has to get to work. She's getting angry that the door is locked."

**Patient Profile:**
- Name: Margaret
- Disease Stage: Middle
- Behavioral Patterns: sundowning, exit-seeking at night, believes she still works as a school teacher
- Calming Strategies: playing Frank Sinatra music, offering warm milk, showing family photo album
- Safety Concerns: fall risk, door locks sometimes left unlocked

**Response:**

### 1. RIGHT NOW (First 60 Seconds)

- Stay calm and step between Margaret and the door gently, without blocking her physically. Make eye contact and say warmly: "Margaret, it sounds like you're ready to get going. Let me help you get ready."
- Try offering her warm milk: "How about we have something warm before you head out? You can't teach on an empty stomach." This buys time and shifts her focus.
- If she stays agitated, put on some Frank Sinatra. Familiar music can interrupt the urgency she is feeling and help her body relax even when her mind cannot.

### 2. WHY THIS IS HAPPENING

In the middle stage of Alzheimer's, Margaret's brain is pulling up old routines that feel absolutely real to her. Her long-term memory of decades of teaching is stronger than her awareness of the current time or her retirement. The nighttime confusion (sundowning) makes this worse because her internal clock is disrupted. She is not being difficult -- she genuinely believes she is late for work and feels the same stress you would feel if you were running late.

### 3. WHAT NOT TO DO

- **Do not say "You don't work anymore" or "You retired years ago."** This contradicts her reality and will feel like someone is gaslighting her. It typically escalates agitation into anger or tears, because she cannot process the correction.
- **Do not physically block the door or grab her arm to stop her.** This can trigger a fight-or-flight response and may lead to her pushing past you or falling. Instead, redirect her attention so she *chooses* to move away from the door.

### 4. WHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

- If Margaret becomes physically aggressive (hitting, throwing objects) and you cannot safely redirect her within 10-15 minutes.
- If she does get outside and you cannot locate her within 5 minutes, call 911 immediately and tell them you have a person with Alzheimer's who has wandered. Give them a physical description and the direction she was heading.
- If this is happening multiple nights in a row, contact Margaret's doctor during business hours to discuss whether a medication review or sleep assessment is needed.

## Example 2: Aggressive Behavior During Bathing (Middle Stage)

**Situation:** "Dad started hitting me when I tried to help him into the shower. He's yelling that I'm trying to hurt him. I'm scared and I don't know what to do. He hasn't bathed in 5 days."

**Patient Profile:**
- Name: James
- Disease Stage: Middle
- Behavioral Patterns: resists personal care, becomes aggressive when feeling vulnerable, calmer in the mornings
- Calming Strategies: talking about his time in the Navy, playing country music, offering coffee
- Safety Concerns: history of hitting during care tasks, unsteady on feet in bathroom

**Response:**

### 1. RIGHT NOW (First 60 Seconds)

- Step back. Right now, the bath is not happening, and that is okay. Put distance between you and James so he feels safe again. Say calmly: "I'm sorry, James. I didn't mean to upset you. Let's take a break."
- Give him 10-15 minutes to calm down. Offer him coffee or turn on some country music in another room. Let his body come down from the fear response before you try anything else.
- When things are calm, you can try a partial cleanup instead -- a warm washcloth wipe-down at the sink can work well and feels much less threatening than a shower.

### 2. WHY THIS IS HAPPENING

In the middle stage of Alzheimer's, James may not fully understand what is happening when you guide him toward the shower. Being undressed by another person can feel frightening and humiliating. His brain interprets your help as a threat, and hitting is his way of protecting himself. This is not anger at you -- it is fear. His history of becoming aggressive during care tasks tells us this is a pattern tied to feeling vulnerable, not a personality change.

### 3. WHAT NOT TO DO

- **Do not insist on the bath right now.** Five days without a full shower is not a medical emergency. Pushing through his resistance teaches his brain that bathtime equals danger, and next time will be even harder. A sponge bath or washcloth cleanup is a perfectly valid alternative.
- **Do not approach from behind or move quickly around him in the bathroom.** Sudden movements in a vulnerable setting amplify his fear response. Always approach from the front, narrate what you are doing ("I'm going to hand you a warm towel"), and let him do as much as he can himself.

### 4. WHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

- If James injured you -- even a minor bruise or scratch -- document it and mention it to his doctor. Escalating aggression during care is something his care team needs to know about.
- If you notice skin breakdown, rashes, or a strong odor that suggests a hygiene-related infection, call his doctor within 24 hours. That changes the urgency calculation.
- If he is aggressive for more than 20 minutes and you cannot de-escalate, call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline (1-800-272-3900) for real-time coaching.

## Example 3: Sundowning Anxiety (Middle Stage)

**Situation:** "It's 4:30pm and Mom is pacing around the house saying she wants to go home. We ARE home. She's been here 30 years. She's getting more and more upset and I can't calm her down."

**Patient Profile:**
- Name: Dorothy
- Disease Stage: Middle
- Behavioral Patterns: sundowning starting around 4pm, asks to "go home" daily, calmer after dinner
- Calming Strategies: folding laundry together, looking at grandchildren's photos, listening to hymns
- Safety Concerns: has tried to leave through back door during sundowning episodes

**Response:**

### 1. RIGHT NOW (First 60 Seconds)

- Do not try to convince Dorothy that she is home. Instead, validate the feeling: "It sounds like you're missing home, Dorothy. Tell me about it -- what do you miss most?" This lets her express the emotion instead of fighting you.
- Bring out some laundry to fold together. The familiar, repetitive motion of folding gives her hands something to do and can ease the pacing. Sit beside her and fold alongside her.
- Check that the back door is secured. While you redirect her attention, quietly make sure her exit routes are safe.

### 2. WHY THIS IS HAPPENING

"Home" for Dorothy probably does not mean this house. In the middle stage of Alzheimer's, "I want to go home" usually means "I want to feel safe and familiar." Her brain is reaching for a feeling -- the comfort of her childhood home, or a time when the world made sense. Sundowning makes this worse in the late afternoon because fatigue, shifting light, and disrupted circadian rhythms all increase confusion and anxiety. This pattern of daily sundowning around 4pm is very common at her stage.

### 3. WHAT NOT TO DO

- **Do not say "You ARE home" or point to familiar objects as proof.** This feels dismissive to Dorothy because "home" is an emotion, not a location. Arguing the facts will make her feel more lost and misunderstood, which increases her distress.
- **Do not try to keep her busy with something unfamiliar or complex.** A new activity requires cognitive processing she does not have right now. Stick with things she knows how to do automatically, like folding laundry or looking at photos.

### 4. WHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

- If Dorothy becomes so distressed that she is crying inconsolably, hyperventilating, or hitting herself for more than 15 minutes despite your best redirection efforts.
- If she gets past you and exits the house, follow at a safe distance and call 911 if you cannot guide her back within a few minutes. Do not chase her -- running can trigger a fall or make her run faster.
- If sundowning episodes are getting longer or more intense week over week, bring a written log of the timing and duration to Dorothy's next doctor appointment. Worsening patterns may signal a need for care plan changes.

## Example 4: Refusing Medication (Late Stage)

**Situation:** "Mom keeps spitting out her pills. I've tried hiding them in applesauce and pudding. She just holds them in her mouth and spits them out when I turn around. She needs these medications. I'm so frustrated."

**Patient Profile:**
- Name: Helen
- Disease Stage: Late
- Behavioral Patterns: difficulty swallowing, resists oral medications, more cooperative in the morning
- Calming Strategies: gentle hand massage, soft spoken words, humming familiar lullabies
- Safety Concerns: aspiration risk, on blood pressure and anti-seizure medication

**Response:**

### 1. RIGHT NOW (First 60 Seconds)

- Take a breath. Step back from the medication battle for this moment. Helen can sense your frustration, and it makes her more resistant. Say gently: "That's okay, Helen. We'll try again in a little bit."
- Try a gentle hand massage while humming to her. Physical comfort can reset the interaction so the next attempt does not carry the tension of the last one.
- When you try again, experiment with timing -- her profile shows she is more cooperative in the morning. If this is an evening dose, ask her doctor if the timing can be shifted.

### 2. WHY THIS IS HAPPENING

In the late stage of Alzheimer's, Helen may be spitting out pills for reasons she cannot tell you. She may have difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), the pills may taste bitter, or she may not understand what the pills are and her instinct is to reject something unfamiliar in her mouth. This is not defiance. Her brain is operating on basic sensory responses: something unexpected is in my mouth, so I spit it out.

### 3. WHAT NOT TO DO

- **Do not force her mouth open or hold her jaw to make her swallow.** This is dangerous -- it increases aspiration risk (the pill going into her lungs instead of her stomach), and it turns medication time into something traumatic that she will fight harder each time.
- **Do not skip doses without talking to her doctor, and do not double up missed doses.** Some medications (especially anti-seizure medication) can be dangerous to stop or change abruptly. Her doctor needs to know she is having difficulty.

### 4. WHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

- Call Helen's doctor or pharmacist tomorrow and ask specifically: "Can any of Helen's medications be converted to liquid, dissolvable, or patch form?" Many medications have alternatives that bypass the swallowing problem entirely.
- If Helen is coughing or choking when she tries to swallow anything (not just pills), call her doctor within 24 hours. This may indicate worsening dysphagia that needs a swallowing evaluation.
- If she has missed multiple doses of her anti-seizure medication, call her doctor or the on-call nurse line today -- do not wait for a regular appointment.

## Example 5: Hallucinations (Middle-Late Stage)

**Situation:** "Dad is talking to people who aren't there. He says there's a woman sitting in the chair and he's having a full conversation with her. He seems happy but it's really scaring me. Should I tell him she's not real?"

**Patient Profile:**
- Name: Walter
- Disease Stage: Middle-late
- Behavioral Patterns: visual hallucinations in the evening, talks to deceased wife, generally pleasant hallucinations
- Calming Strategies: watching nature documentaries, eating ice cream, looking at bird feeder outside window
- Safety Concerns: sometimes tries to set a place at the table for the hallucinated person, occasionally becomes upset when the "visitor" leaves

**Response:**

### 1. RIGHT NOW (First 60 Seconds)

- Do not interrupt the conversation. Walter is calm and happy right now, and that is a good thing. There is no crisis to solve in this moment.
- If you want to gently engage, you can say: "It sounds like you have nice company, Walter. Who are you talking to?" Let him share. This is not "encouraging" hallucinations -- it is meeting him where he is.
- Check the environment: sometimes reflections in windows or mirrors at night, or shadows from the TV, can trigger visual hallucinations. Adjust lighting if you notice a pattern.

### 2. WHY THIS IS HAPPENING

In the middle-to-late stage of Alzheimer's, the parts of Walter's brain that process visual information and distinguish memory from reality are damaged. He may be seeing his deceased wife because that memory is deeply embedded and feels current to him. His brain fills in what it expects to see. These pleasant hallucinations are very common at this stage and are different from the hallucinations of delirium (which tend to be frightening). When he talks to his wife, he may be experiencing genuine comfort.

### 3. WHAT NOT TO DO

- **Do not say "There's nobody there" or "She's not real."** This will not make the hallucination stop -- it will make Walter feel confused, embarrassed, or frightened. He sees her as clearly as you see the furniture. Being told his eyes are lying is deeply disorienting.
- **Do not play along with specifics you cannot see.** You do not need to pretend you see the person too. A neutral response like "That sounds nice" respects his experience without requiring you to lie.

### 4. WHEN TO CALL FOR HELP

- If the hallucinations shift from pleasant to frightening -- Walter sees threatening people, insects, or becomes panicked -- call his doctor within 24 hours. Frightening hallucinations may indicate a medication side effect, infection (especially UTI), or delirium.
- If he becomes aggressive toward you because you are "interfering" with his visitor, step out of the room and give him space. Call the Alzheimer's Association Helpline (1-800-272-3900) if you need guidance in the moment.
- If the hallucinations are accompanied by a sudden change in alertness, fever, or a fall, call 911. Sudden onset of hallucinations with other symptoms can indicate a medical emergency like infection or stroke.

## Usage Notes for Prompt Engineering

These examples demonstrate the following patterns that the system prompt should enforce:

1. **Patient name usage**: The patient's first name appears in nearly every section, personalizing the guidance and reminding the caregiver to think of the patient as a person, not a problem.

2. **Disease stage grounding**: The "Why This Is Happening" section always connects the behavior to the specific disease stage, helping caregivers understand that the behavior is neurological, not intentional.

3. **Calming strategy integration**: At least one strategy from the patient profile appears in the "Right Now" section, making the guidance feel personalized rather than generic.

4. **Counter-intuitive "What NOT to Do"**: Every example includes guidance that contradicts common caregiver instincts (arguing reality, insisting on care, correcting hallucinations). This is the most valuable section.

5. **Specific escalation criteria**: Time-bounded ("within 24 hours"), condition-specific ("if accompanied by fever"), and action-oriented ("call her doctor and ask specifically..."). Never vague "call if it gets worse."

6. **Tone consistency**: Short sentences. Direct but warm. No jargon. No blame. Acknowledges the caregiver's fear and frustration without dwelling on it.

7. **Safety guardrail compliance**: No reality contradiction, no medication advice (always defers to doctor), no restraint suggestions, no clinical diagnosis language.
